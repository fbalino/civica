Most constitutions don't grow old. The standard estimate, from a study of every national constitution written between 1789 and 2005, puts the median lifespan at about 19 years — roughly the gap between one generation and the next. Half of all constitutions are dead before they reach drinking age in the country that wrote them.

That figure comes from *The Endurance of National Constitutions*, the 2009 book by Zachary Elkins, Tom Ginsburg, and James Melton that built the first comprehensive dataset on constitutional mortality. They tracked some 900 documents across more than 200 years. The pattern is lopsided: a small number of constitutions endure for a century or more, while the typical one is replaced, suspended, or torn up within two decades.

Survival isn't random, though. The same research found that three design choices, present at the moment of drafting, do most of the work in predicting whether a constitution will still be standing a generation later.

## The benchmark cases

The United States Constitution went into effect in 1789 and is the oldest single-document national constitution still in force. That makes it the outlier people reach for, but it's worth being precise about why it has lasted. It is short — about 7,500 words including its 27 amendments — and it has been formally changed only 27 times in 236 years, ten of those in the first two years as the Bill of Rights. Most of its adaptation has happened through judicial interpretation rather than rewriting.

Compare that with the documents at the other end of the table. The Dominican Republic has adopted more constitutions than any other country: the one ratified in 2010 was its thirty-ninth since independence in 1844. Venezuela has had 26, Haiti 24, Ecuador 20. Thailand has cycled through 20 charters since it abolished absolute monarchy in 1932, most of them replaced after one of the country's 13 coups. In those countries the constitution is closer to a press release from whoever currently holds power than a durable framework.

## A workable amendment path

The first thing that keeps a constitution alive is the ability to change it without breaking it. You might expect a sacred founding text to be hard to touch, but Elkins, Ginsburg, and Melton found the opposite. A constitution that cannot be amended at a reasonable pace falls out of step with the country it governs, and the pressure that builds up tends to get released by scrapping the whole thing rather than editing it.

India is the working example. Its 1950 constitution is the longest in force anywhere — about 145,000 words — and it has been amended 106 times as of early 2026. That looks chaotic next to the American 27, but the frequent amendment is precisely what has kept the document relevant through land reform, the creation of new states, tax overhauls, and the addition of fundamental duties. The text bends so the framework survives.

Japan sits at the opposite pole and survives anyway. Its 1947 constitution, drafted under American occupation, has never been amended once — the oldest unamended constitution in the world. Its famous Article 9, renouncing war, has been stretched by reinterpretation to permit self-defense forces, but the words themselves are untouched. Japan shows that rigidity can work, though it usually doesn't; the country has leaned on interpretation to do what amendment does elsewhere.

More amendments aren't inherently better. What matters is that a constitution has *some* credible path to change. When the only way to update the rules is to overthrow them, you get the Dominican Republic's thirty-nine.

## Inclusion at the drafting table

The second factor is who writes the thing. Constitutions produced behind closed doors by a narrow faction tend to die when that faction loses power. Constitutions drafted through a broad, participatory process build a wider constituency with a stake in keeping them.

South Africa's 1996 constitution is the case study comparative scholars cite most. It came out of a multi-party negotiation with extensive public consultation — the constitutional assembly ran a public-comment campaign that drew roughly two million submissions before the text was finalized. The result has a deep reservoir of legitimacy that an imposed document would lack, and it has held through three decades and several changes of government.

Inclusion also explains some failures. Constitutions written quickly by a victorious party or a single leader — often to entrench that leader — carry the seeds of their own replacement. When the balance of power shifts, the new winners see no reason to honor a deal they were never part of.

## Specificity, in the right dose

The third factor is harder to summarize because the relationship runs both ways. A constitution that is too vague gives courts and politicians nothing to anchor to, and disputes spill outside the document. A constitution that is too detailed becomes brittle — it legislates so many specifics that ordinary policy disagreements turn into constitutional crises, and the text needs constant amendment or wholesale replacement to keep up.

The endurance research finds that a relatively specific text helps, up to a point, because clear rules reduce the ambiguity that fuels disputes and give actors a shared reference. But the same detail that makes a constitution useful can make it fragile when it tries to settle questions that should be left to ordinary lawmaking. India's 145,000 words and 106 amendments are partly the product of a very detailed text that has to be edited often. The American 7,500 words leave more to interpretation and need editing less.

Brazil's 1988 constitution illustrates the trade-off. At over 60,000 words it specifies everything from pension formulas to the standard length of the school day, which means routine policy fights become constitutional amendment fights — it has been amended well over 100 times since 1988. The document survives, but it does so by being amended almost as a matter of routine.

## What it adds up to

Put the three together and the durable constitution has a recognizable shape: a clear but not exhaustive text, written through a process broad enough to bind more than the winning side, with amendment rules loose enough that the country can update its rules without overthrowing them. None of this guarantees survival — a deep enough crisis kills constitutions regardless of design, and the 19-year median is driven heavily by coups, wars, and state collapse rather than bad drafting alone. But design tilts the odds, and the tilt is measurable.

It also reframes what a long-lived constitution actually is. The US document is far from perfect, but it endures because it is short enough to interpret, was ratified through a contested public process, and can be amended when the pressure is high enough. The countries on their twentieth or thirty-ninth constitution usually got one or more of those wrong.
