Democracies used to die on a single afternoon. Tanks in the boulevards, a colonel reading a communiqué, the airport closed. That still happens — Niger in July 2023, Gabon a month later — but it is no longer the main story. The countries that have lost the most democratic ground this century did it without a coup, without suspending the constitution, often without breaking a single law on the day it mattered. They held elections the whole time.

Political scientists have a tidy name for the method: autocratic legalism. The idea is that a freely elected government uses its lawful majority to rewrite the rules so that losing power becomes very hard. Courts, election administrators, public broadcasters, the civil service, the prosecutor's office — each is captured one at a time, through legislation that looks technical and dull. By the time the pattern is obvious, the institutions that might have stopped it answer to the people doing it.

The V-Dem Institute's 2025 report counted 45 countries in active episodes of autocratization, against 19 democratizing. For the first time in over two decades, autocracies outnumber democracies. Most of those declines were not seizures of power. They were slow.

## The playbook has a fixed sequence

The mechanics repeat across very different countries because the same institutions stand in the way everywhere. The order tends to be the same too.

First, the courts. A captured supreme or constitutional court is the master key — it can bless everything that follows. Hungary's Fidesz government, after winning a two-thirds parliamentary majority in 2010, lowered the mandatory retirement age for judges from 70 to 62 in a single step. The change removed roughly 10% of the country's judges in its first year and let the government appoint replacements, including a quarter of the Supreme Court (Kúria) bench and nearly half the county court presidents. Both Hungary's own Constitutional Court and the Court of Justice of the European Union later ruled the measure unlawful. By then most of those judges were already gone, and there was no clean route back to their old jobs. A ruling that arrives after the chairs are filled is not a remedy.

Venezuela ran the same move more bluntly. In December 2004 the Chávez-aligned National Assembly passed a law enlarging the Supreme Tribunal from 20 justices to 32 and filled the new seats with loyalists. Human Rights Watch flagged it at the time as court-packing. The effect held for years: studies of the court's output found it did not rule against the executive in tens of thousands of decisions after the expansion. In 2015, when the opposition won a legislative supermajority, the outgoing pro-government assembly rushed through 13 more friendly justices before handing over the keys. That stacked court went on to neuter the new legislature and, in 2020, to appoint a compliant National Electoral Council.

## After the courts, the referee and the megaphone

Once the judiciary is safe, the next targets are the bodies that decide who wins elections and the outlets that tell voters what happened.

Election administration rarely makes headlines, which is part of the appeal. Redistricting, registration rules, the composition of the electoral commission, who counts the ballots — all of it can be reshaped by ordinary statute. Hungary redrew its constituency map and rewrote its electoral law so that Fidesz could win two-thirds of parliamentary seats on roughly half the vote. The elections stayed real in the sense that ballots were counted honestly; the field was tilted before anyone voted. The Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe has repeatedly called Hungary's contests free but not fair.

Media capture is the quieter half. The model is not censorship by decree but ownership and money. In Hungary, allied businessmen bought up outlets and in 2018 donated around 500 of them into a single pro-government foundation, KESMA, run with state advertising and friendly regulators. Independent estimates put the share of Hungarian media under direct or indirect government influence near 80% by the mid-2020s. A voter can still buy a critical newspaper. Most never see one.

Turkey reached the same destination on a faster track. The 2016 coup attempt became the pretext: about 4,400 judges and prosecutors were dismissed and replaced, roughly a quarter of the bench. A 2017 referendum then abolished the prime minister's office and concentrated executive power in the presidency, and gave the president and parliament control over the Board of Judges and Prosecutors that hires and disciplines the judiciary. The endpoint became visible in March 2025, when Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu — the leading opposition figure — was arrested on the very day his party prepared to nominate him for president, with prosecutors floating sentences in the thousands of years. By then the courts that might have checked the arrest were staffed by people the executive had chosen.

## Why it's so hard to see in time

Each individual step is defensible on its own terms. Retirement ages need adjusting. Court sizes are set by law. Media markets consolidate everywhere. Anti-terror powers exist in every democracy. Defenders of each measure can point to a precedent in some healthy democracy and not be lying. The damage is cumulative and directional, and it only resolves into a picture once several moves have landed.

That is also why the warning signs are structural rather than dramatic. A few worth watching:

- A high court that stops ruling against the government on anything that touches the government's hold on power.
- Changes to election rules, districting, or commission membership pushed through by the incumbent shortly before a vote.
- Public broadcasters and large private outlets changing ownership toward government allies, paired with state advertising as a lever.
- Emergency or anti-terror powers that outlive the emergency and get pointed at opposition figures.

## Poland shows the exit is narrow

Erosion is not a one-way ratchet; governments do get voted out and try to repair the damage. But reversal turns out to be far harder than the original capture.

Poland's Law and Justice party (PiS) spent 2015 to 2023 running the playbook: a Constitutional Tribunal packed with loyalists, a politicized National Council of the Judiciary, the public broadcaster turned into a campaign instrument. The opposition coalition under Donald Tusk won the October 2023 election and took office that December, promising to restore the rule of law and unlock frozen EU funds.

A government can win an election and still find the captured institutions blocking the repair. Parliament passed laws in 2024 to reset the Constitutional Tribunal and reform the judicial council; the president, a PiS ally, referred them to that same captured Tribunal, which struck them down. The institutions built to be hard to remove are, in fact, hard to remove — even by a fresh majority with a popular mandate. Poland's restoration is real but partial, and it has been slow, contested, and legally messy at every step.

The throughline across Hungary, Turkey, Venezuela, and Poland is unglamorous. No tanks, no juntas, mostly no single illegal act you could point a camera at. The decisive battles were fought over the retirement age of judges, the wording of an electoral statute, the ownership of a regional newspaper. That is precisely what makes the pattern dangerous to a free press and a busy electorate: the moves are boring, technical, and easy to wave through one at a time. Catching backsliding early means treating dull institutional changes as the main event, because by the time it looks like a crisis, the referees have already been replaced.
