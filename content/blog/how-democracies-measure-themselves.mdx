Ask four respected institutions to score the same country's democracy and you will get four different answers — on four different scales, derived by four different methods. The Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) put the United States at 7.65 out of 10 in its 2025 Democracy Index, calling it a "flawed democracy." Freedom House gave the US 81 out of 100 the same year and labeled it "Free." V-Dem, the Varieties of Democracy project, still classed it among "liberal democracies." Same country, three different verdicts.

That spread isn't sloppiness. It's what happens when you measure something abstract — the quality of a political system — with instruments built to answer slightly different questions. Once you know what each index actually counts, the disagreement stops looking like error and starts looking like the point.

## The four big rulers

Start with the scales themselves, because they are not interchangeable.

**Freedom House** publishes *Freedom in the World*, scoring 0 to 100 across 25 indicators grouped into political rights and civil liberties. Its annual country-and-territory series runs back to 1972. Countries land in three buckets: Free, Partly Free, Not Free. Freedom House also flags which states qualify as "electoral democracies," a narrower test than the headline score.

**The EIU Democracy Index** runs 0 to 10 across five categories: electoral process and pluralism, functioning of government, political participation, political culture, and civil liberties. Its global series begins in 2006 and sorts countries into full democracy, flawed democracy, hybrid regime, and authoritarian regime. In the 2024 edition, 25 countries were classified as full democracies and 60 as authoritarian regimes.

**V-Dem** is the academic heavyweight: a 0-to-1 continuous index built from a large country-year dataset extending back to 1789. Its electoral democracy index is one of several; it also publishes liberal, participatory, deliberative, and egalitarian indices, each capturing a different theory of what democracy is for.

**Polity** scored regimes from −10 (full autocracy) to +10 (consolidated democracy) and was, for decades, the default in political-science papers. It is also the cautionary tale here, which we'll come back to.

## Expert coders versus the box-score

The scale matters less than who does the scoring.

V-Dem leans heavily on **expert coders** — scholars and country specialists, with multiple coders assigned across topics. To stop one unusually pessimistic or optimistic coder from dragging a score, V-Dem runs the raw judgments through a statistical measurement model that accounts for coder reliability and stated uncertainty, then publishes an estimate alongside upper and lower bounds. The output is a modeled distribution, not a single confident dot.

Freedom House and the EIU also use experts, but through more centralized research processes. Their analysts synthesize public-opinion evidence, news reporting, NGO findings, and field research into each project's declared scoring framework.

Polity sat at the other end: a small in-house team coding institutional features — how the chief executive is selected, what constrains them, how open political participation is — from the academic and news record. That made it reproducible and transparent, but blunt. It graded the machinery of a regime more than the lived texture of rights.

This is the real fault line in the field, and it has a name: subjective expert judgment versus "objective" institutional indicators. Expert coding captures things a checklist misses — whether elections are genuinely competitive, whether courts actually constrain a president, whether journalists can work without fear. The cost is that experts can drift. A live methodological debate, argued by Andrew Little and Anne Meng on one side and V-Dem's Carl Henrik Knutsen and colleagues on the other, asks whether expert coders worldwide have grown systematically gloomier over the past decade — which would manufacture a "democratic decline" partly out of mood. Knutsen's team, applying item-response theory and bias tests, reports no evidence of such time-varying bias. The dispute is unresolved, and it matters: if the coders are drifting, every expert-coded trend line inherits the drift.

## Where they actually disagree

The divergence shows up most where you'd least expect it — among the safest democracies.

Our World in Data's comparison notes that the indices cannot even agree on whether Denmark or Norway is the more democratic, or whether Chile is more democratic now than a decade ago. On the United States, the three measures parted ways in 2025: EIU's "flawed democracy" at 7.65 (its lowest US score since the index began in 2006), Freedom House's "Free" at 81, V-Dem's "liberal democracy." Each is internally consistent. They simply weight different things — the EIU is more sensitive to polarization and trust in institutions, Freedom House to the formal architecture of rights, V-Dem to a broad bundle of expert-assessed components.

None of these indices is simply right and the others wrong. A democracy score is a measurement *with a method attached*, and the method travels with the number. Reporting "Country X scored 6.2" without saying who scored it, on what scale, and against which definition is like quoting a temperature without saying Celsius or Fahrenheit.

## The politics of being ranked

Scores are not neutral artifacts once they're published. Governments lobby, complain, and occasionally retaliate. A poor ranking gets cited in foreign-ministry statements and opposition press releases; a good one gets framed and hung on the wall. Because the EIU, Freedom House, and V-Dem are private or academic bodies rather than treaty organizations, they have no enforcement power — only reputational weight. That weight is exactly why being ranked is contentious.

Then there is **Polity**, the field's quiet warning. For years it was the standard regime measure in comparative politics. Its active coding has since wound down, and large parts of the research community migrated to V-Dem precisely because the older project couldn't keep pace with the granularity scholars wanted. An index is only as durable as the institution maintaining it. A score frozen in 2018 is a historical document, not a current reading — a distinction easy to forget when the number is still floating around in spreadsheets and citations.

## How the Civica Index reads them

[The Civica Index](/civica-index) does not try to crown one of these measures. It treats them as instruments, each with a domain it reads well, and combines them deliberately. The structural backbone of the index draws on the same family of sources — V-Dem, Freedom House, the World Bank's governance indicators, and others — each entering at its documented vintage and carrying its own provenance, so a reader can trace any component back to the source row that produced it.

Two habits from this field carry directly into our methodology. First, every score travels with its method: a Civica figure is meant to be cited *with* its sources, not stripped of them. Second, uncertainty is part of the data, in the spirit of V-Dem's published confidence bounds — a point estimate that hides its own error bars is a false comfort.

For regime classification we lean on externally attested taxonomies — the V-Dem Regimes of the World scheme and the Bjørnskov-Rode / CGV dataset — rather than inventing a Civica vocabulary, so the labels inherit their sources' academic standing instead of asking you to trust ours. You can see how any two countries' scores and classifications line up on [compare](/compare), check the underlying structural facts in the [factbook](/factbook), or look up an unfamiliar term in the [glossary](/glossary).

The indices themselves keep arriving at the same honest summary: measuring democracy is disciplined estimation, closer to a survey with error bars than to a thermometer reading. Four serious projects can study one country and report different numbers because each asks a slightly different question. The work worth doing is showing your instruments, which is why every number on Civica is built to be opened up and checked.
