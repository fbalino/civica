A term limit is a deadline written into a constitution: serve your two terms, then go. It is a simple rule and a fragile one, because it is only as durable as the people who count the days, and those people usually work for the president.

The United States wrote the modern template. After Franklin Roosevelt won four elections, Congress passed the Twenty-Second Amendment in 1951, capping the presidency at two elected terms. Most presidential republics built since then copied the idea. By recent counts, the large majority of presidential and semi-presidential systems carry some term cap; the roughly one-sixth that don't are mostly autocracies. So the limit is the norm. The harder case is the popular incumbent who reaches the deadline and decides it does not apply to him.

## What term limits are actually for

The point of a hard cap is to force turnover even when the incumbent is winning. Elections alone won't do it. A leader who controls the courts, the media, and the electoral machinery can keep winning votes that mean less each cycle. A term limit removes that option by fiat and forces a country to practice the thing fragile democracies are worst at: handing power to a rival and surviving it.

That is also why the limit is the first thing an ambitious incumbent attacks. Removing it is the cleanest way to convert a temporary mandate into an indefinite one without ever losing an election.

## The playbook is short

For all the local color, the methods sort into a handful of moves. A leader rarely invents a new one; he picks from the menu.

- **Amend the constitution.** Change the text so the cap rises or vanishes.
- **Have a friendly court reinterpret it.** Leave the text alone; rule that it never meant what everyone thought.
- **Win a referendum.** Take the question to voters and let a managed campaign do the work.
- **Zero the clock.** Pass a new constitution or amendment and declare that past terms no longer count.

Most of the famous cases combine two or three of these.

## Xi Jinping: just delete the line

The bluntest version belongs to China. In March 2018 the National People's Congress voted to strike the two-term limit on the state presidency from the constitution. The tally was 2,958 in favor, two against, three abstaining. Tibet's delegation and the rest fell in line; with the press and internet tightly censored, there was almost no visible debate. The same package wrote "Xi Jinping Thought" into the constitution and created new "supervisory commissions" to investigate officials. The limit that had governed the office since the post-Mao reforms was gone in a single afternoon, clearing the way for Xi to stay past the end of his second term in 2023.

It worked because China is a one-party state where the legislature ratifies rather than deliberates. Where an incumbent can't simply order the change, he has to be more inventive.

## Vladimir Putin: zero the clock

Russia's 2020 amendments are the textbook example of the fourth move. The constitution still said two consecutive terms. Rather than abolish the cap outright, the new text declared that Putin's earlier terms would not be counted — the clock was reset to zero. He could therefore run twice more, legally, extending his potential tenure to 2036.

The maneuver was wrapped in a much larger package — a ban on same-sex marriage, "faith in God," the primacy of Russian law over international law, a new State Council — and put to a nationwide vote held over a week in summer 2020. It passed with 77.9 percent reported in favor, amid widespread accounts of coercion and multiple voting. The "zeroing" provision, the one change that actually mattered for power, barely featured in the official campaign at all. The genius of the design was its modesty: the cap survived on paper, so the government could claim it had changed nothing.

## Nayib Bukele: capture the court

El Salvador shows the third move done with unusual speed. The constitution barred consecutive reelection in at least six separate articles. In May 2021 the legislature, controlled by Bukele's party, removed the sitting magistrates of the Constitutional Chamber and the attorney general and installed replacements. In September the new judges ruled that the ban applied only to a previous president, not the current one — so the incumbent could run again after all. Bukele won reelection and began a fresh term on 1 June 2024. In July 2025 the assembly went further, approving an amendment to allow indefinite reelection and stretching the term from five years to six. The text had not been wrong; it had simply been overruled by judges who owed their seats to the man it constrained.

Bolivia tried the same court route and the comparison is instructive. Evo Morales lost a 2016 referendum on a fourth term, 51.3 percent against. So his party asked the constitutional court instead, which in 2017 declared that term limits violated the candidate's "human rights" under the American Convention. The court cleared him to run; the wider crisis that followed in 2019 showed how brittle that kind of victory can be.

## Africa: the slow grind

Sub-Saharan Africa produced the largest catalog of attempts, and the most varied. Researchers at the Africa Center for Strategic Studies count more than thirty presidents since 2000 who tried to push past their limits, with around twenty succeeding.

- **Uganda.** Yoweri Museveni had the two-term limit lifted in 2005, then the presidential age cap of 75 removed in 2017, clearing both fences in turn.
- **Rwanda.** A 2015 referendum let Paul Kagame take a third seven-year term and then two more five-year terms, a path that could keep him in office to 2034.
- **Côte d'Ivoire.** Alassane Ouattara argued that a new 2016 constitution reset his count, letting him run again in 2020 — the zeroing argument, transplanted to Abidjan.
- **Burundi.** Pierre Nkurunziza claimed in 2015 that his first term, since it came via parliament rather than direct election, did not count. His third-term bid set off mass violence; a 2018 constitution later stretched terms from five years to seven.

A pattern in the data is worth flagging: where the legal route to a third term is blocked, the alternative is often a coup. The Africa Center argues that term-limit evasions and military takeovers feed the same instability, two outcomes of the same refusal to leave on schedule.

## Why the trick keeps working

The common thread is that none of these leaders broke the law in any way a court would say so. They changed the law, or changed the judges, or changed the count. A term limit binds the future but it is enforced by courts and electoral boards that exist in the present, so a leader who already controls those bodies can rewrite the deadline before it arrives. The cap that looks ironclad on the page is only as good as the institutions willing to apply it to the man in office.

That is also why the limit is a useful diagnostic for outsiders. A government that respects an inconvenient deadline is showing real restraint; one that engineers its way around the deadline, by referendum or court ruling or zeroing the count, is showing how it will treat every other constraint too. The men in this piece all kept holding elections. What they stopped honoring was the date on which they had promised to leave.
