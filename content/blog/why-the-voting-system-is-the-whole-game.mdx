Two countries can hold the same election — same voters, same parties, same totals — and end up with completely different parliaments. The thing that decides which one you get is the counting rule. Before any policy is debated, the choice of how to turn votes into seats has already shaped how many parties survive, whether one of them can govern alone, and which voters end up represented by no one at all.

It is one of the most consequential design choices a democracy makes, and one of the dullest to discuss. It rarely makes the news, because it only changes once or twice a century. When it does change, the whole character of a country's politics shifts with it.

## First-past-the-post and the squeeze on small parties

The simplest system is also the most distorting. Under first-past-the-post (FPTP), the country is carved into single-member districts, and whoever gets the most votes in each one wins the seat. There is no prize for second place, and no national tally that matters.

The 2015 UK general election is the cleanest illustration on record. The UK Independence Party won 12.6% of the national vote — more than the Liberal Democrats and the Scottish National Party combined — and took exactly one seat in the 650-member House of Commons. The SNP, with under 5% of the UK-wide vote, won 56 seats, because its support was packed into Scotland where it finished first in 56 of 59 districts. Geography, not total support, did the work.

This is what the political scientist Maurice Duverger described in 1951. He argued that single-member plurality elections tend toward two dominant parties through two pressures. The mechanical one is just arithmetic: a party that comes second everywhere earns zero seats. The psychological one follows from it — voters who can see their preferred small party has no chance of finishing first defect to a larger party they can stomach, which starves the small party further. The pattern that results is now called [Duverger's law](/glossary).

The law is a tendency, not a guarantee. The United States, with its single-member House districts, is a textbook two-party case. But Canada and India both use FPTP and run busy multiparty systems, because regionally concentrated parties — the Bloc Québécois, India's many state parties — clear the bar in their home turf the way the SNP does in Scotland. FPTP punishes parties whose voters are spread thin and rewards parties whose voters are bunched together.

## Proportional representation and the price of inclusion

Proportional representation (PR) flips the priority. The aim is to make the share of seats match the share of votes. Win 10% of the vote, take roughly 10% of the seats. Smaller parties survive because finishing first in a particular place is no longer the test.

The trade-off is fragmentation, and Israel is the standard example of the pure version. The Knesset's 120 seats are allocated by nationwide proportional representation, so a party needs only a small slice of the national vote to enter parliament. No party has ever won an outright majority in an Israeli election. Every government is a coalition, often a fragile one — between 2019 and 2022 the country held five elections in under four years. Israel raised its electoral threshold to 3.25% in 2014 specifically to thin out the smallest parties and make coalitions easier to build, and even that has not produced durable majorities.

The threshold is the dial that every PR system tunes. Set it low and you get many parties and unstable coalitions; set it high and you protect stability at the cost of shutting out smaller movements. Most PR democracies land somewhere in the middle, and where they land tells you a lot about what they were most afraid of when they wrote the rule.

## The mixed-member compromise

The third path tries to capture both virtues at once. Mixed-member proportional (MMP) systems give each voter two votes: one for a local representative in a district, and one for a party. District seats are filled by FPTP, but the party vote determines each party's overall share of the legislature, with extra list seats added to top up parties that won fewer districts than their national vote warranted. You keep a local member to call, and the final parliament still looks roughly proportional.

Germany has run a version of this since 1949, and its mechanics show both the appeal and the headaches. The 5% threshold keeps fringe parties out. But topping up underrepresented parties created "overhang" seats, which steadily inflated the chamber: the Bundestag's nominal size is 598, yet it ballooned to 709 members after the 2017 election and 736 after 2021. A 2023 reform capped it at 630 by abolishing overhang seats outright, tightening the party vote into the sole determinant of a party's seat count.

New Zealand offers the rare case of a country switching systems on purpose and being able to compare before and after. After decades of FPTP delivering lopsided results, voters approved MMP in a binding referendum on 6 November 1993, with 53.9% in favour. The first MMP election followed in 1996. The effect was immediate and durable: New Zealand went from a tidy two-party Parliament to a permanent multiparty one. Its threshold is 5% of the party vote *or* one electorate seat, a side door that lets a party with a strong local base into Parliament even if its national vote falls short. After the 2023 election the National Party governed in a three-way coalition with ACT and New Zealand First — the kind of arrangement that was almost unthinkable under the old rules.

## What the rule is really choosing

Underneath the machinery, every electoral system is settling the same small set of choices: how closely seats should track votes, how many parties should be able to survive, how much weight local representation should carry against national proportionality, and whether governing should usually mean one party in charge or several at the table.

No setting is neutral, because each one favours a different value. FPTP buys decisive single-party governments at the cost of representing minorities poorly. PR buys faithful representation at the cost of slower, coalition-bound governing. MMP splits the difference and pays for it in complexity. A reformer who claims their preferred system is simply fairer is usually smuggling in a preference about which trade-off matters most.

This is also why electoral systems are so hard to change. The parties that win under the current rule are, by definition, the ones with the power to rewrite it, and they rarely volunteer to make their own next election harder. New Zealand managed it only because public anger at two successive governments forced a referendum the politicians could not control. Most countries never get that moment, which means the counting rule they happen to have — often inherited from a colonial power or chosen hastily at a founding — keeps shaping their politics long after anyone remembers deciding on it.
