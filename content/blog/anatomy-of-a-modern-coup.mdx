In the space of three years, soldiers took control of five governments in West and Central Africa. Mali went first, in August 2020, then again in May 2021. Guinea followed in September 2021. Burkina Faso had two coups in a single year, January and September 2022. Niger fell in July 2023, Gabon a month later. Six successful seizures, five countries, one stretch of the map that analysts now flatly call the "coup belt."

These were not the coups of the 1960s and 70s, with their midnight radio broadcasts and tank columns improvised on the spot. The takeovers of the 2020s have a recognizable shape. They are carried out by the units closest to the president, justified in near-identical language, and followed by a sequence of moves so consistent that you can almost predict the press releases. Reading the six side by side is the quickest way to see how power changes hands by force.

## The people who do it are already inside the palace

The defining feature of the recent wave is proximity. The officers who seized power were not rebels marching from a frontier garrison. They were the men charged with guarding the head of state.

In Niger, President Mohamed Bazoum was detained on 26 July 2023 by the presidential guard, the elite unit responsible for his personal security. Its commander, General Abdourahamane Tchiani, declared himself leader of a new junta within days. Reporting at the time noted that Bazoum had reportedly been preparing to replace Tchiani — a reminder that the trigger is often a threat to the guard's own position, not an abstract crisis of state.

Gabon ran on the same logic. When the military overthrew Ali Bongo on 30 August 2023, the man who emerged on top was General Brice Oligui Nguema, head of the Republican Guard and a cousin of the deposed president. He had been a bodyguard to Bongo's father. The dynasty was undone by its own household troops.

Mali's first coup in 2020 came from the special forces. Burkina Faso's two coups in 2022 came from within the regular officer corps, each led by men who had been entrusted with the fight against the insurgency. The pattern holds: the closer a unit sits to the centre of power, the better placed it is to take it.

## The script almost never changes

Strip the coups of their local detail and the public justification is remarkably uniform. In each case the new rulers cited the same three failings of the government they had just removed: insecurity, corruption, and the loss of national sovereignty.

The security argument has real weight in the Sahel. Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger sit at the centre of a jihadist insurgency linked to al-Qaeda and Islamic State affiliates that has killed thousands and displaced millions. Burkina Faso's September 2022 coup was launched explicitly on the grounds that Paul-Henri Damiba — himself installed by a coup eight months earlier — had failed to turn back the insurgents. Captain Ibrahim Traoré, who replaced him, was one of the officers who had backed the January takeover and grown disillusioned. A coup justified by insecurity, undone by continued insecurity, eight months apart.

The sovereignty argument is where the recent wave breaks most sharply from the past. The juntas framed their seizures as a rupture with France, the former colonial power, and with what they cast as a Western-aligned regional order. Mali's second coup in May 2021 turned in part on exactly this question — whether to keep cooperating with France or pivot toward Russia. By the end of that year, Wagner Group mercenaries were deploying across the country, and by mid-2022 France had withdrawn its Operation Barkhane troops.

## What usually follows: a transition that doesn't transition

Every junta promises a return to civilian rule. The promise has a poor record.

The standard move is to announce a "transition period" with a timeline, then let the timeline slip. Mali's 2020 coup installed a civilian figurehead, Bah Ndaw, as interim president, with Colonel Assimi Goïta as his deputy. When Ndaw and his prime minister tried to assert independence in May 2021, Goïta had them detained and stripped of power, then took the presidency himself. The civilian veneer lasted nine months.

Gabon offers the cleaner illustration of the endgame. Oligui Nguema promised free and credible elections after seizing power in 2023. He held them in April 2025 — and won, formalizing his rule through the ballot box. A takeover that began as the overthrow of a rigged election ended with the man who led it elected president.

## Why they cluster

Coups are contagious, and the 2020s wave shows why. A successful seizure next door lowers the perceived cost of attempting one at home. It demonstrates that the regional and international response — condemnation, sanctions, suspension from blocs — can be survived.

The clustering became institutional. In September 2023, facing an ECOWAS threat of military intervention to reverse the Niger coup, the three Sahelian juntas signed a mutual defence pact, the Alliance of Sahel States. An attack on one would be treated as an attack on all. In January 2024 the three announced they were leaving ECOWAS entirely, and on 6 July 2024 they upgraded their pact into a formal confederation. The regional body built to deter coups had, in effect, lost a third of its core membership to them.

ECOWAS suspended each member after its takeover, hoping isolation would raise the price of seizing power. Instead the three juntas walked out together and built a rival bloc, turning the threat of expulsion into a shared cause.

## What it means for reading a government

A coup is rarely a single event. It is the visible moment in a longer erosion — of civilian control over the security services, of the credibility of elections, of the bargain that keeps soldiers in barracks. The Sahel wave is extreme, but the warning signs it ran through are general: a presidential guard that has grown into a praetorian faction, elections whose results are disputed before they are counted, a security crisis that no civilian government can be seen to solve.

That is why structure matters as much as the headline. A country's [government type](/glossary), the independence of its courts, the rules governing its armed forces — these are the load-bearing walls. When they are sound, a disgruntled colonel is a problem; when they are hollow, he is a head of state by Friday. You can trace each of these countries' formal systems and their current standing in the [factbook](/factbook), and compare how their governance scores moved before and after the seizures in [the Civica Index](/civica-index) and on [compare](/compare).

The men who took power across the Sahel all claimed they were rescuing their countries from collapse. Several years on, the insurgencies that justified the takeovers are no smaller, the promised elections have mostly arrived late or not at all, and the region's democratic institutions are weaker than when the wave began. A coup of this kind is quick to stage and easy to recognize. Undoing one — restoring civilian control, credible elections, courts that hold — takes far longer, and on current evidence may not happen at all.
