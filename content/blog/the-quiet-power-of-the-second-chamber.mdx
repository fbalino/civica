A second chamber is the part of a legislature most citizens could not name and most governments wish would behave. Roughly four in ten countries run two chambers; the rest get by with one. Among the bicameral set, the label "upper house" hides an enormous range of real power. Germany's Bundesrat can stop a whole class of federal laws cold. The British House of Lords, by statute, can delay an ordinary bill for about a year and then must give way. Both are upper houses. They are not doing remotely the same job.

What separates a Bundesrat from a House of Lords is how much the second chamber can insist. Comparative scholars sort bicameral systems along two axes: how symmetric the two chambers' powers are, and how differently the two chambers are composed. A second chamber matters when it is both powerful and elected (or appointed) on a different basis than the first — that combination is what lets it block the lower house rather than merely echo it.

## Symmetric, asymmetric, and the gap in between

In a symmetric system, both chambers must agree for a law to pass, and the first chamber cannot easily overrule the second. The United States Senate is the clearest example. A federal statute needs the consent of both the House of Representatives and the Senate; neither can override the other. There is no constitutional mechanism by which the House passes a bill over the Senate's objection. The two chambers are co-equal on legislation, which is why divided control of Congress so reliably produces stalemate.

In an asymmetric system, the second chamber can slow legislation but not finally stop it. The House of Lords is the textbook case. Under the Parliament Act 1911, the Lords lost the power to veto money bills and could delay other legislation for up to two years; the Parliament Act 1949 cut that delay to about one year. On top of the statute sits the Salisbury Convention, an understanding dating from 1945 that the Lords will not block legislation promised in the governing party's election manifesto. The elected Commons has the last word, and everyone in the building knows it.

Most bicameral countries fall between these poles. The second chamber has real teeth on some subjects and is overridable on others, so the detail that matters is the boundary line: which bills the upper house can actually kill.

## The Bundesrat: a second chamber made of governments

Germany draws that boundary unusually sharply, and its upper house is strange enough to deserve its own description. The Bundesrat is not elected by voters at all. Its 69 votes belong to the sixteen state (Land) governments, which appoint and instruct their delegations; the most populous states such as North Rhine-Westphalia and Bavaria control six votes each, the smallest three. A state's votes must be cast as a bloc. When a Land's coalition cannot agree how to vote, it typically abstains, which in the Bundesrat counts against a measure that needs consent.

German law splits in two. Bills classified as "consent laws" (Zustimmungsgesetze) — broadly, those that reach into how the states administer or finance federal policy — cannot pass without the Bundesrat's agreement, which takes at least 35 of the 69 votes. On these, the Bundesrat holds an absolute veto: if it refuses, the bill is dead, no matter how large the Bundestag's majority. Everything else is an "objection law" (Einspruchsgesetz), where the Bundesrat can lodge an objection that the Bundestag can then override by a matching majority. When the two chambers deadlock, a joint Mediation Committee (Vermittlungsausschuss) of sixteen members from each side tries to broker a text both can accept.

The practical effect is that when opposition parties control enough state governments, they can hold up a chunk of the national legislative program through the back door of the Bundesrat. That has happened often enough to shape German politics: a chancellor with a Bundestag majority but a hostile Bundesrat governs on a short leash. The chamber is the institutional spine of German federalism, which is the point — it exists to make the states co-author the laws they will be made to carry out.

## Why upper houses exist

Across all the variety, second chambers tend to serve one or more of three purposes.

The first is territorial representation. Federations almost always use the upper house to give component states a voice that does not scale with population. The US Senate gives Wyoming (under 600,000 people) the same two seats as California (around 39 million), a deliberate result of the 1787 Connecticut Compromise that traded equal state representation in the Senate for population-based seats in the House. Australia, Switzerland, and Germany all build their upper chambers around the same federal logic.

The second is revision. The House of Lords spends much of its time on detailed line-by-line scrutiny of bills the Commons has passed in a hurry, sending back amendments the government can accept or, if it has the votes and the will, reverse. An unelected, expert, less partisan chamber is good at this slow work even though — or because — it cannot ultimately impose its view.

The third is restraint. A second chamber with a different electoral cycle or selection method acts as a brake on the lower house's majority, forcing a measure to clear two differently composed bodies. The countries that abolished their upper houses concluded the brake cost more than it was worth.

## The case for one chamber

A long list of democracies looked at their second chamber and removed it. New Zealand abolished its appointed Legislative Council in 1951; the council had been so easily packed with government supporters that it had stopped functioning as an independent check. Denmark scrapped its upper house, the Landsting, in a 1953 constitutional referendum after concluding the two chambers had become near-duplicates. Sweden went unicameral in 1971 after two commissions of inquiry argued there were strong democratic reasons to concentrate authority in a single elected chamber. Iceland, Croatia, Estonia, Hungary, Peru, Venezuela, and others have made the same move at various points.

The arguments repeat across countries. If the second chamber merely mirrors the first, it adds delay without adding judgment. If it diverges sharply from the first, it can frustrate an elected majority without a clear democratic mandate of its own — the recurring complaint about appointed or hereditary chambers. The House of Lords is still working through that critique: the House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026 removed the last 92 hereditary peers from the chamber in April 2026, the largest change to its composition since 1999, and the question of whether the whole house should be elected remains open.

Even powerful upper houses face a softer version of the same pressure. The US Senate's 60-vote cloture threshold — the practical bar for ending a filibuster and moving most legislation, in place since the Senate lowered it from two-thirds in 1975 — turns the chamber into a supermajority body for ordinary business, and periodic fights over scrapping it (most recently during the 2025 government shutdown) are arguments about exactly how much one chamber should be able to stop.

## Reading a country's bicameralism

The label on the door tells you little. To know what a second chamber actually does, three things are worth checking: whether both chambers must consent or one can override the other; how the upper chamber is selected, since a differently chosen body is the one capable of genuine resistance; and which subjects, if any, it can veto outright rather than merely delay. Germany answers all three in a way that makes the Bundesrat formidable on the issues it cares about. Britain answers them in a way that makes the Lords influential but ultimately deferential. The United States built a Senate that can match the House blow for blow.
