A useful question to ask about any country is a blunt one: when something goes wrong, whose phone rings first? The answer is rarely the person on the postage stamp. In most of the world the job of *representing* the state and the job of *running* it have been split between two people, and the split is where a lot of confusion about world politics begins.

The two roles have names. The **head of state** is the country's official embodiment — the figure who signs treaties, receives ambassadors, and stands for national continuity. The **head of government** is the chief executive who sets policy, runs the ministries, and answers for what the administration actually does. Sometimes one person holds both jobs. Often they don't, and telling them apart explains why a king can reign without ruling and why a president can be enormously visible yet constitutionally weak.

## When the jobs are fused

The United States is the cleanest case of fusion. The president is head of state and head of government at once — receiving foreign leaders in the morning and directing the executive branch in the afternoon. There is no separate prime minister to share the load or absorb the blame. This is the defining feature of a **presidential system**: a single, directly mandated executive who serves a fixed term and cannot be dismissed by the legislature over ordinary policy disputes.

That concentration is the system's strength and its hazard. The president owns the executive branch outright, but a hostile Congress can stall the agenda for years, and there's no constitutional release valve short of impeachment. You see the same architecture across most of the Americas — Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Indonesia — though each layers its own habits on top of the shared blueprint.

## When the jobs are split

Walk across to the parliamentary world and the picture flips. The head of state recedes; the head of government takes the wheel.

In the United Kingdom the monarch is head of state and reigns over a great deal of pageantry while making no open political decisions. Real authority sits with the prime minister — Keir Starmer since 5 July 2024 — who governs only as long as the House of Commons will have him. The monarch's powers are formally vast and practically dormant; the prime minister's powers are informally enormous and entirely contingent on a majority.

Germany draws the same line with a written constitution instead of convention. The **Federal President** is head of state and largely ceremonial, while the **Chancellor** is the chief executive — so dominant that political scientists describe the country as a "chancellor democracy." The Chancellor can't be removed by a simple no-confidence vote. Under the Basic Law's *constructive vote of no confidence*, the Bundestag can only oust a chancellor by simultaneously electing a replacement. That mechanism, designed to avoid the revolving-door governments of the Weimar era, has succeeded exactly once: in October 1982, when Helmut Kohl replaced Helmut Schmidt. A 1972 attempt against Willy Brandt failed by two votes.

The lesson of the parliamentary world is that the impressive title and the operative power often live in different buildings. A ceremonial head of state can dissolve parliament, swear in governments, and command the armed forces on paper, while a prime minister who lost a vote last week is the one actually deciding whether the country goes to war.

## The hybrid: France and the art of cohabitation

Some countries refuse to choose, and the result is the **semi-presidential system** — a directly elected president *and* a prime minister who depends on the legislature. France's Fifth Republic is the textbook example, and its history is the best illustration of what happens when the two halves don't agree.

When the president's party controls the National Assembly, the president dominates and the prime minister executes. But when voters hand the Assembly to the opposition, the president must appoint a prime minister he didn't choose, from a party he opposes. The French call this **cohabitation**, and it has happened three times: François Mitterrand with Jacques Chirac (1986–1988), Mitterrand with Édouard Balladur (1993–1995), and Chirac with Lionel Jospin (1997–2002). The arrangement settled into a rough division of labor — the president steering foreign affairs and defense, the prime minister running domestic and economic policy.

Cohabitation was awkward enough that France tried to engineer it away. In 2000 a referendum cut the presidential term from seven years to five, aligning it with the Assembly's five-year cycle so that presidential and legislative majorities would usually be elected together.

The fix mostly worked, until 2024. After his alliance was beaten in that June's European elections, President Emmanuel Macron dissolved the Assembly and called a snap vote. The result was a parliament split three ways with no majority: the left-wing New Popular Front took 180 of 577 seats, Macron's centrist bloc 159, and the far-right National Rally 142. With no opposing party holding a majority, France got something stranger than classic cohabitation — a string of fragile minority governments. Michel Barnier's fell to a no-confidence motion on 4 December 2024, the first French government toppled that way since 1962 and the shortest-lived of the Fifth Republic. François Bayrou followed, then Sébastien Lecornu, who briefly resigned and was reappointed. The presidency stayed put; the head-of-government chair became a hot seat.

## The committee that runs a country

The rarest arrangement does away with a single chief executive entirely. Switzerland is governed by the **Federal Council**, a body of seven members who together serve as the collective head of state and head of government. There is no single president in the ordinary sense.

There is a title called President of the Swiss Confederation, but it rotates among the seven councillors every year and confers no extra power. The holder is *primus inter pares* — first among equals — chairing meetings and handling some ceremonial duties while keeping the ministry he or she already runs. For 2026 the office is held by Guy Parmelin, with Ignazio Cassis as vice president and slated to take the chair in 2027. Decisions are made by the council as a whole, and once made they're defended collectively, even by councillors who argued the other way. This is the **directorial** model, and Switzerland is its most stable practitioner.

It's a deliberate rejection of the strong-executive instinct that shapes almost everywhere else. Power is spread so thin that no single official can dominate, which suits a country built on cantonal bargaining and frequent referendums.

## Why the distinction is worth keeping straight

Knowing which chair holds the power changes how you read the news. The president hosting a state visit may be someone who can't pass a budget without the legislature's consent. The prime minister with a one-seat majority may still control defense, the cabinet, and the legislative calendar day to day. That gap between title and authority is usually written into the constitution on purpose, not a quirk.
