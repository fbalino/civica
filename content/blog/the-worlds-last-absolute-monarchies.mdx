Most of the world's monarchs do almost nothing. King Charles III opens Parliament by reading a speech his ministers wrote, signs laws he cannot refuse, and stays out of politics on pain of constitutional crisis. Japan's Emperor Naruhito has even less: Article 1 of Japan's 1947 constitution names him "the symbol of the State," and the document gives him no governmental power at all — not even nominal command of the Self-Defense Forces.

Then there are the kings who still run the place. In a small set of countries, the monarch is the executive: he appoints the cabinet, controls the budget, commands the army, and faces no legislature that can remove him. Governing versus reigning is the distinction that splits the world's roughly 40 monarchies into two very different categories.

## Where the king still governs

By the standard count, four states are genuine absolute monarchies in 2026: Saudi Arabia, Brunei, Oman, and Eswatini. Each works differently, but in all four the crown is the government, not a figurehead presiding over one.

**Saudi Arabia** has no elected national legislature and no constitution in the conventional sense; the Quran and the Sunna serve that role, alongside the 1992 Basic Law. King Salman, 90, has largely withdrawn from public life, and his son Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has been the effective ruler since 2017, a grip formalised when he was named prime minister in September 2022. The Consultative Assembly (Majlis al-Shura) has 150 members — all appointed, none elected — and advises rather than legislates.

**Brunei** is governed under a state of emergency that has been continuously in force since 1962. Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah, on the throne since 1967, is the longest-reigning living monarch of any sovereign state — a title he inherited when Denmark's Margrethe II abdicated in January 2024. He simultaneously holds the posts of prime minister, defence minister, finance minister, and foreign minister. The 33-member Legislative Council is appointed and has no power to make law.

**Oman** changed sultans in January 2020, when Haitham bin Tariq succeeded Qaboos bin Said, who had ruled for nearly fifty years without ever publicly naming an heir. Haitham closed that gap fast: a 2021 royal decree created the country's first formal crown-prince office and fixed succession on the sultan's eldest son, making Theyazin bin Haitham the designated heir. The same overhaul of the Basic Statute reaffirmed certain civil freedoms on paper. The elected Consultative Council can review some legislation but cannot govern.

**Eswatini** is the last absolute monarchy in Africa. King Mswati III has ruled since 1986; political parties are barred from contesting elections, and the king appoints the prime minister, a share of parliament, and the senior judiciary. The arrangement has a second, older head — the Ndlovukati, or Queen Mother — which leads some scholars to describe the system as a diarchy on paper, even if power in practice runs through the king.

## The Gulf's in-between cases

Qatar and the United Arab Emirates are usually filed under "mixed" rather than "absolute," because each has a representative body of some kind. The bodies exist, but they cannot constrain the ruler.

Qatar is the sharpest illustration. In 2021 it held the first elections in its history for the Shura Council, choosing 30 of 45 members. The experiment lasted one cycle. Emir Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani argued the vote had inflamed tribal and family rivalries, and in 2024 a referendum returned the council to a fully appointed body. The opening lasted three years.

The UAE's Federal National Council has 40 members, half of them chosen through a limited electoral college and — by a 2019 rule — half of its seats reserved for women. It advises and reviews; it does not legislate, and political parties do not exist. Kuwait, often cited as the Gulf's liveliest parliament, shows the ceiling of that model: in May 2024 the emir suspended the elected National Assembly entirely, citing chronic gridlock, and assumed its powers.

## What ceremony actually changed

The ceremonial crowns of Europe and Japan were not always weak. They were disarmed, deliberately and in writing, and the contrast with the governing monarchies is mostly a contrast of constitutions.

Japan's emperor lost his powers in the 1947 occupation constitution, which placed sovereignty with the people and reduced the throne to a symbol. The United Kingdom never wrote it down in one document, but the convention is just as binding: the sovereign acts only on ministerial advice, and the last royal refusal of assent to a bill was in 1708. Across Scandinavia the pattern repeats — Sweden's 1974 Instrument of Government stripped the monarch of every formal political function, leaving him the most constitutionally powerless head of state in Europe. The crowns kept their ceremonial role and lost every formal power.

That is why "monarchy" is close to useless as an analytical label on its own. Sweden and Saudi Arabia are both monarchies and have almost nothing in common about how they are governed. The governing-versus-reigning split, not the presence of a crown, is what tracks with civil liberties, rule of law, and how power changes hands. ## Succession is the stress test

A ceremonial monarchy can survive a bad king, because a bad king cannot do much. An absolute monarchy is more fragile, because the king is the state, and a handover puts the whole structure at risk at once.

This is why Oman's 2021 succession decree mattered out of proportion to its length. Qaboos ruled for half a century and left no named heir, and the sultanate spent his final years braced for a vacuum. Saudi Arabia faces its own first: when Mohammed bin Salman takes the throne, it will be the first father-to-son succession since 1953, breaking the brother-to-brother pattern the founding king's many sons had relied on for seventy years. Brunei's sultan is nearing sixty years on the throne with a designated successor in his eldest son, but no governing institution exists independently of him to absorb the shock if the transfer goes wrong.

Reform pressure adds the other variable. Eswatini's monarchy faced its gravest challenge in June 2021, when pro-democracy protests were met with lethal force; the killing in January 2023 of the human-rights lawyer Thulani Maseko, and later sedition convictions of two reformist legislators, showed how far the crown will go to keep the lid on. The promised reforms that looked imminent after 2021 have stalled. Where there is no mechanism to share power, the only available answers to dissent are concession or repression, and absolute monarchies have generally preferred the latter.

Kings who merely reign keep multiplying as Europe's constitutions hold. The four who actually govern are each one contested succession away from finding out whether the institution outlasts the man.
