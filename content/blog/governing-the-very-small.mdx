San Marino swears in two new heads of state every six months. On 1 April and 1 October, the Grand and General Council elects a fresh pair of Captains Regent, who serve together for half a year and then step aside. The arrangement is not a gimmick. It dates to at least 1243, and the logic behind it has held for nearly eight centuries: in a republic of roughly 34,000 people, leaving one person at the top for too long is the surest way to let personal power crowd out the council. Once a regent's term ends, they cannot serve again for three years.

That is the first thing scale does to a government. When the whole country could fit inside a large football stadium, the distance between rulers and ruled collapses, and the institutions that survive are the ones built to stop any single faction or family from capturing the state.

The world has about three dozen states under a million people, and a handful under 100,000. None of them is just a small version of a big country. Their governments look odd from the outside and make complete sense from the inside.

## The institutions are bigger than the country

A country of 40,000 people still needs a parliament, a court system, a foreign ministry, a treasury, and someone to negotiate with the European Union. The work does not shrink in proportion to the population.

Liechtenstein, with around 40,000 residents, elects a 25-seat parliament, the Landtag, by proportional representation from two constituencies — Oberland gets 15 seats, Unterland gets 10. That is one legislator for roughly every 1,600 people. A country the size of Germany at the same ratio would seat tens of thousands. Microstate legislatures are dense by necessity: the institution has a fixed minimum size, and the population can't dilute it.

Liechtenstein also shows the second adaptation. It doesn't try to staff a full diplomatic corps. Under a 1919 agreement, Swiss ambassadors represent Liechtenstein abroad unless Vaduz chooses to send its own, and the two countries have shared a customs and currency union since 1924. Liechtenstein joined the United Nations in its own right in 1990, but it borrows Switzerland's reach for the day-to-day work of being a state. You keep sovereignty where it counts and rent the rest.

## Shared plumbing

If your country is too small to run its own central bank, you join someone else's.

Six independent Eastern Caribbean states — Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica, Grenada, St Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, and St Vincent and the Grenadines — plus the British territories of Anguilla and Montserrat share a single currency, the Eastern Caribbean dollar, issued by the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank in St Kitts. The currency has been pegged at 2.7 to the US dollar since 1976, and the bank has run monetary policy for the whole union since 1983. Nine territories also share one judiciary, the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court, with judges who ride circuit between islands.

This is the microstate answer to fixed costs. A central bank, a supreme court, and a credible currency peg are expensive to build and hard to run well alone. Pool them across a dozen small members and each one gets institutions it could never afford on its own. The trade is autonomy: you don't set your own interest rate, and your highest court sits partly in someone else's jurisdiction. For a small open economy, the stability has generally been judged worth the cost.

## What the very small can't outsource

A small state can borrow a central bank or a court. It cannot borrow higher ground.

Tuvalu, nine low-lying atolls in the central Pacific with roughly 11,000 people, has watched the sea rise about 21 centimetres in 30 years — close to twice the global average. Some projections put most of the country underwater by 2100. So the government has started doing something no state has done before: backing itself up. At the 2022 UN climate conference, Tuvalu's then–foreign minister Simon Kofe announced a plan to create a "digital nation," scanning the islands in 3D and moving the machinery of government to the cloud so that statehood — laws, records, recognition — could outlive the physical territory. Tuvalu has since amended its constitution to assert that its statehood is permanent even if the land disappears, and signed a 2023 treaty with Australia offering a migration pathway to its citizens.

It is a genuinely new question in international law. A state is conventionally defined by a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter relations with other states. Tuvalu is testing whether the territory clause is load-bearing, or whether a government and a people can carry a country forward without dry land under it.

## When a single resource is the whole economy

Nauru, an eight-square-kilometre island with around 12,000 people, is the cautionary tale every small state knows. After independence in 1968 it sat on some of the richest phosphate deposits on Earth, and for a stretch it posted among the highest incomes per person in the world. The mining stripped roughly 80% of the island's interior to bare pinnacles of rock, the deposits ran down by the early 2000s, and a national trust meant to outlast the phosphate was drained by bad investments and worse management.

What replaced it is a candid portrait of how a tiny state stays solvent without an economy of its own: Australian aid tied to an offshore asylum-processing centre, fishing-licence fees from foreign tuna fleets, and the sale of citizenship. In 2025 Nauru reopened a "golden passport" program — citizenship for about US$105,000 — explicitly to fund relocating residents away from a rising coastline, with the government projecting the scheme could supply close to a fifth of its revenue. Sovereignty, for a state this small, is partly a set of things it can legally sell: passports, fishing rights, banking registration, a vote in international bodies.

## The other extreme: power in one pair of hands

Not every microstate spreads power thin. The two smallest in Europe concentrate it almost entirely.

Vatican City, about 44 hectares and fewer than 900 residents, is the smallest state in the world by area and population and the only absolute monarchy left in Europe. The Pope holds supreme legislative, executive, and judicial power, delegating administration to a Governorate and a Pontifical Commission. There is no separation of powers because there is, in the end, one office.

Liechtenstein moved toward concentration by vote. In a 2003 referendum, just over 64% of voters approved a package giving Prince Hans-Adam II the power to dismiss the government, veto legislation, and shape judicial appointments. The Council of Europe's Venice Commission had warned the reforms were out of step with European democratic norms; the prince had said that if they failed he would decamp to Austria. The result is a rare hybrid — strong hereditary monarch and active direct democracy in the same small country, with citizens holding the formal power to abolish the monarchy and choosing, so far, not to.

Scale doesn't dictate which way a microstate leans. San Marino fragments power across rotating regents and a council; the Vatican fuses it into one elected-for-life monarch. What scale does is make the choice unusually visible. In a country of 34,000, the constitution is the set of rules a few thousand people personally live inside, not a distant abstraction.

## What the small teach the large

The temptation is to treat microstates as curiosities. They are better read as controlled experiments. Each one isolates a variable that bigger states bury under sheer size.

San Marino isolates rotation in office. The Eastern Caribbean isolates pooled institutions. Tuvalu isolates the question of what a state minimally is. Nauru isolates resource dependence and the long bill that comes due after a boom. Vatican City and Liechtenstein isolate how much power one person can hold before a polity stops calling itself a democracy.
