Electoral Systems

How different countries translate votes into representation

First Past the Post
single-member plurality · winner-take-all
How It Works
The country is divided into single-member districts. Voters cast one vote for a candidate. The candidate with the most votes in each district wins the seat, regardless of whether they received a majority.
Key Characteristic
Tends to produce two-party systems (Duverger's Law) and strong single-party governments. Votes for losing candidates have no impact on seat allocation.

Proportional Representation
party-list PR · D'Hondt · Sainte-Laguë
How It Works
Voters choose a party (or candidates on a party list). Seats are allocated in proportion to each party's share of the total vote. Thresholds (typically 3–5%) prevent extreme fragmentation.
Key Characteristic
Produces multi-party systems and coalition governments. Most common electoral system worldwide. Small parties can win seats.

Mixed Member Proportional
additional member system · two-vote system
How It Works
Voters cast two votes: one for a local district candidate (FPTP) and one for a party list (PR). Local seats go to district winners. "Top-up" list seats are added so total seat allocation matches the party vote share.
Key Characteristic
Combines local representation with proportional outcomes. Germany introduced it in 1949 to prevent both Weimar-era fragmentation and single-party dominance.

Ranked Choice Voting
instant runoff · preferential voting · alternative vote
How It Works
Voters rank candidates in order of preference (1st, 2nd, 3rd…). If no candidate has a majority, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their votes redistributed to voters' next preference. Repeat until a candidate reaches 50%+1.
Key Characteristic
Guarantees the winner has majority support. Reduces strategic voting. Used nationally in Australia since 1918 and adopted for presidential elections in several smaller democracies.

Two-Round System
runoff voting · ballotage · second round
How It Works
If no candidate wins a majority (50%+1) in the first round, the top two candidates proceed to a runoff election. The runoff winner takes the seat. Most common for presidential elections.
Key Characteristic
Ensures every winner can claim majority legitimacy. The gap between rounds (usually 2–3 weeks) allows for coalition building and vote transfer negotiations.
