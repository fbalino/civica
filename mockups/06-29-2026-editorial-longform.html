Inside Democratic Resilience

By The Civica Editorial Desk June 29, 2026 14 min read

When institutions bend rather than break, the story is rarely told in a single headline. It accumulates — in courts that hold, in elections that proceed, in a press that keeps printing. This is an account of the quieter machinery that lets some democracies absorb shocks that would topple others.

Resilience is an unfashionable word in the study of government. It promises neither the drama of collapse nor the triumph of reform, and it resists the neat scoring that rankings demand. Yet across the eighteen months we have tracked through the Civica Index and the daily Civica Pulse, no single quality has separated the steady from the slipping more reliably than the capacity of a state's institutions to take a blow and keep functioning.

Consider the ordinary week of a constitutional court. It hears a case challenging an emergency decree; it rules; the ruling is obeyed. Nothing about this is guaranteed. In the countries where our governance signals have deteriorated most sharply, it is precisely this last step — the obeying — that quietly stops happening. The decree stands. The court is ignored, then reorganized, then staffed with the compliant. By the time the decline registers in an annual democracy score, the institution that might have arrested it has already been hollowed out.

“Backsliding is rarely a single act. It is the steady substitution of loyalty for competence, one appointment at a time.”

— from the Civica Index methodology notes

What, then, makes an institution hard to hollow? Our reading of the evidence points to three reinforcing properties, and the strongest democracies tend to hold all three at once. The first is redundancy — overlapping checks so that no single capture is decisive. The second is professional independence: a civil service, judiciary, and electoral administration whose careers do not depend on pleasing the executive of the day. The third, and most fragile, is public expectation — the widely shared sense that the rules are not optional.

The arithmetic of overlapping checks

Redundancy sounds inefficient, and in a narrow sense it is. Why fund an ombudsman, an auditor-general, a constitutional court, and an independent electoral commission when their mandates partly overlap? The answer is that overlap is the point. An aspiring strongman who can capture one body still faces three more, each able to sound an alarm the others amplify.

The Pulse data bears this out at the level of the daily event. In states with dense, overlapping oversight, a single adverse event — a contested appointment, a leaked surveillance program — tends to be met within days by a countervailing one: a court injunction, a parliamentary inquiry, a regulator's refusal. In states where oversight has thinned, the same adverse event drifts, uncontested, into the new normal.

Independence is a career, not a clause

Constitutions are generous with the word “independent.” The harder question is whether independence is something a person can actually afford. A judge who can be transferred to a remote posting, an auditor whose budget is set by the audited, an election official serving at the pleasure of a partisan minister — each is independent on paper and dependent in practice.

This is why the most durable systems invest in dull-sounding guarantees: fixed terms, staggered appointments, security of tenure, salaries that cannot be cut as punishment. They convert independence from an aspiration into an incentive. The countries that have climbed in our governance signals over the past year did not, for the most part, pass sweeping reforms. They defended these undramatic protections when they came under pressure.

What resilience cannot do

It would be a mistake to read resilience as a guarantee. Institutions that absorb shocks can still be ground down by a patient, sustained assault — and a public that has come to expect the rules can, over a generation, be taught to expect less. Resilience buys time and raises the cost of capture; it does not abolish the threat. The value of tracking it daily, as the Pulse attempts to, is not prophecy but early warning: a way to see the substitution of loyalty for competence while it is still one appointment at a time.
